The Soccer Factor Model, explained

How Andorra and Göbel separate a forward from his team, and what we changed to fit their model to public data

An account of the Soccer Factor Model (Andorra & Göbel, 2024), our four-skill season-level version of it, how we checked it and what it found.

This page explains the model behind the explorer that Wyatt and I built. The data dictionary describes the data. The code is in src/sfm/ and scripts/, and running the scripts rebuilds every number below.

Skill and the team around it

A striker’s goal count measures the striker and his team at once. A team that wins the ball high and plays most games against weaker sides feeds its forward more chances, and better ones, than a team fighting relegation. Andorra and Göbel (2024) call the raw count observed performance, OP. They model it as a function of the player’s own skill, \(\alpha\), and his team’s performance, TP:

\[\text{OP} = g(\alpha, \text{TP}; \theta).\]

A scout wants to know whether the player carries the team or the team carries the player. To answer that, you have to pull \(\alpha\) out of OP.

The idea comes from finance. A fund manager who beats the market may have picked good stocks. Or he may have held the kinds of stock that rose that year, small firms say, or cheap ones. Finance separates the two by regressing the manager’s returns on these market-wide factors. The intercept, the return the factors cannot explain, is the manager’s alpha. The Soccer Factor Model treats team strength as the factors. Whatever is left of the player’s performance is his alpha.

The model in the paper

The paper follows 144 strikers in England, Germany, Italy and Spain from 2000/01 to 2023/24. That is about 33,000 player-matches, scraped from the German magazine kicker.

The outcome is goals per player per match. Only 1.5% of player-matches produce three goals or more, so the authors cap the count at three. That leaves four ordered categories, 0, 1, 2 and 3+. They model these with an ordered logistic likelihood, which turns a single latent score into a probability for each category by way of a set of cutpoints. The first cutpoint is fixed so that the model is identified.

Two factors stand for team strength. One marks home games. The other is the gap in league points between the player’s team and its opponent so far that season. Both are known before kickoff, so no later information leaks into the prediction. The authors tried more factors and found that the points gap had already absorbed them. Adding correlated factors would only have muddied the coefficients.

Skill in the paper has three parts:

\[\alpha_{i,m,s} = \underbrace{\mu_b + \delta_{b,i}}_{\text{talent}} + \underbrace{h^W(D_{i,m,s})}_{\text{within-season}} + \underbrace{h^C(N^S_{i,s})}_{\text{cross-season}}.\]

The first part is talent: a common level \(\mu_b\) plus a player effect \(\delta_{b,i}\), with the player effects summing to zero. The second, \(h^W\), tracks form within a season. Its input is the number of days since the player’s first match of the season. It adds a short-lengthscale curve to a medium one, so it can pick up a hot streak as well as slow fatigue. The third, \(h^C\), follows the career. Its input is the number of seasons the player has been in the sample.

Each curve is a Gaussian process. An exact Gaussian process slows to a crawl with tens of thousands of matches. The paper therefore uses a Hilbert-space approximation, or HSGP, which builds each curve from a fixed set of basis functions under a Matérn-5/2 kernel.

To compare players, the authors borrow baseball’s wins above replacement. Performance above replacement, PAR, sets a player’s predicted goals against those of a pool of ordinary forwards. Skill above replacement, SAR, makes the same comparison with every team factor set to zero, as though all teams were equally strong. The difference, SAR − PAR, measures how far the team inflated or deflated the player’s numbers.

Messi comes out as the most skilled striker of the period. Haaland’s estimate is high and very uncertain, because his career is short. The three most skilled players, Messi, Haaland and Ronaldo, also gained most from their teams. Skill peaks about four seasons into a career. In leave-one-out cross-validation, the full model predicts better than two simpler versions of itself.

What we changed

We wanted to fit the paper’s ideas to data anyone can download. We used Understat for the top five leagues from 2014/15 to 2023/24, taken from a Kaggle archive, and match results from football-data.co.uk.

The archive holds every match and every shot, but no lineups. When a forward plays without taking a shot or making a key pass, his appearance leaves no trace. That happens in 29% of appearances. The paper’s per-match model needs those empty games, so we could not fit it as written. Season totals, minutes included, are complete. We fitted the model to player-seasons instead, and used the change to measure four skills where the paper measures one.

Four skills

Goals per 90 minutes split exactly into three ratios:

\[\frac{\text{goals}}{90} = \frac{\text{shots}}{90}\times\frac{\text{xG}}{\text{shot}}\times\frac{\text{goals}}{\text{xG}}.\]

Expected goals, or xG, is the probability that a shot like the one taken goes in. Each ratio is a separate ability. The first counts how often a forward gets a shot away. Wyatt calls a forward who is good at this a Shooter. The second measures where the shots come from: the Fox. The third asks whether he scores more than an average player would from the same chances: the Sniper. We added a fourth skill, the Playmaker, which counts the shots a forward sets up for team-mates. It adds nothing to his own goals, but it is plainly part of a forward’s game.

Each player \(p\) has four correlated skills, with an LKJ prior on their correlations:

\[\alpha_p \sim \text{MVN}(0, \Sigma), \qquad \Sigma = \text{diag}(\sigma)\,\Omega\,\text{diag}(\sigma), \quad \Omega \sim \text{LKJ}(2).\]

For player-season \(r\) and skill \(k\), the model forms a linear predictor. It does the work of the paper’s \(g(\cdot)\):

\[\eta_{k,r} = \mu_k + \lambda_{k,\ell(r)} + \beta_k\,\text{TS}_r + h_k(N^S_r) + \alpha_{p(r),k}.\]

Here \(\lambda_{k,\ell}\) is a league effect, and the five league effects sum to zero. \(\text{TS}_r\) is the team factor: the team’s points per game that season, less the league average. \(h_k\) is a Matérn-5/2 HSGP over seasons observed. Each skill has its own likelihood:

Skill Data Likelihood
Shooter non-penalty shots \(\text{NegBin}\big(\tfrac{\text{min}}{90}e^{\eta_\text{shoot}},\ \phi_s\big)\)
Fox non-penalty xG per shot \(\log(\text{xG}/\text{shot}) \sim N\big(\eta_\text{fox},\ \sigma_f/\sqrt{\text{shots}}\big)\)
Sniper non-penalty goals \(\text{Poisson}\big(\text{npxG}\cdot e^{\eta_\text{sniper}}\big)\)
Playmaker key passes \(\text{NegBin}\big(\tfrac{\text{min}}{90}e^{\eta_\text{create}},\ \phi_c\big)\)

The standard deviation for the Fox shrinks with the square root of the number of shots. A season of 120 shots counts for more than a season of 12. The Sniper uses the season’s xG as its baseline, which makes the skill a ratio of goals to xG.

Multiplying the first three skills gives a forward’s scoring rate on a level field. The league is average, the team is average, and he is at the average point of a career:

\[\text{rate}^{\text{level}}_p = e^{\mu_\text{shoot}+\alpha_{p,\text{shoot}}}\,e^{\mu_\text{fox}+\alpha_{p,\text{fox}}}\,e^{\mu_\text{sniper}+\alpha_{p,\text{sniper}}}\]

Adding \(\beta_k\,\text{TS}\) for each of his real seasons, weighted by minutes, gives his rate with his own teams. SAR and PAR subtract the same rates for the replacement pool, which holds the forwards between the 40th and 60th percentiles of level-field skill. The explorer lets you choose a different pool. League and career stage stay at their averages in both measures. SAR − PAR therefore differs only through the team factor, as in the paper.

The table sets out where we depart from the paper.

Paper Here
Unit player-match player-season (no public lineups)
Outcome goals, ordered logistic shots, xG/shot, goals/xG, key passes
Skill one \(\alpha\) per player four correlated \(\alpha\)s (LKJ)
Team factor home + pre-match points gap season points per game vs league average
Career curves per player, within- and cross-season HSGPs one population HSGP per skill, over seasons
Penalties included excluded
Players 144 hand-picked strikers 593 forwards (≥ 6,000 minutes, plus a few of Wyatt’s picks)

Checking the model

We checked the priors before fitting. Seasons simulated from the priors alone had a median of 39 shots, against 42 in the data. Their 90% range ran from 6 to 169, against 10 to 100. Weak priors should be wider than the data and on the same scale, and these were.

Next we simulated a whole dataset from the model, with known skills, correlations, team effects and a humped career curve, and fitted the model to it. The first fit produced 498 divergences. The intercepts \(\mu_k\) could trade off against the overall level of each career curve, so the sampler wandered along a ridge. Centring each curve’s basis functions over the data removed the ridge, and divergences fell to 16.

The 83% intervals then covered 80% of the true Shooter values, 82% for the Fox, 78% for the Sniper and 83% for the Playmaker. The spreads, the correlations and the career curve came back close to the truth. Finishing was the exception. Its posterior means correlated only 0.56 with the true values, against 0.95 for each of the other skills. The simulation told us, before we saw real data, that finishing would be hard to measure.

The real fit ran 4 chains of 1,500 draws in nutpie, with target_accept = 0.95. It produced no divergences. The largest \(\hat R\), 1.028, belongs to the correlation between Sniper and Playmaker. That correlation also has the smallest effective sample size, about 70. Everything else mixed well.

Posterior predictive checks

Our likelihoods have little in common with the paper’s, so convergence is not enough. The model has to reproduce the data it was fitted to. For each check we draw replicated datasets from the posterior predictive distribution and set them beside what the players actually did. These checks use the same data the model was fitted on, so they can expose misfit but cannot prove the model predicts new seasons well.

ppc = FileAttachment("web/ppc.json").json()
OUT = ["np_shots", "key_passes", "log_xg_per_shot", "npg"]
INK = "#0b0b0b"
REP = "#2a78d6"
FLAG = "#eb6834"
panelW = Math.max(260, Math.min(420, (width - 24) / (width > 640 ? 2 : 1)))

The first check compares whole distributions. The grey bars show the observed player-seasons. Each thin blue line is one of 40 datasets the model generated.

html`<div style="display:flex;flex-wrap:wrap;gap:12px">${OUT.map(v => {
  const d = ppc.distributions[v];
  const bins = d.observed.map((c, k) => ({x1: d.edges[k], x2: d.edges[k + 1], c}));
  const mid = k => d.log_x ? Math.sqrt(d.edges[k] * d.edges[k + 1]) : (d.edges[k] + d.edges[k + 1]) / 2;
  const reps = d.replicates.flatMap((r, j) => r.map((c, k) => ({rep: j, x: mid(k), c})));
  return Plot.plot({
    width: panelW, height: 200, marginLeft: 44, title: d.label,
    x: {type: d.log_x ? "log" : "linear", label: null, tickFormat: d.log_x ? (t => t.toFixed(2)) : undefined, ticks: 5},
    y: {label: "↑ seasons", grid: true},
    marks: [
      Plot.rectY(bins, {x1: "x1", x2: "x2", y: "c", fill: "#c9c8c3", inset: 0.5}),
      Plot.line(reps, {x: "x", y: "c", z: "rep", stroke: REP, strokeOpacity: 0.25, strokeWidth: 1}),
      Plot.ruleY([0])
    ]
  });
})}</div>`

The replicated datasets match the observed distributions for all four outcomes, including the long right tails of shots and goals.

The second check asks whether each observed season looks like a typical draw from its own predictive distribution. For every player-season we compute the share of replicated values below the observed one, the probability integral transform, or PIT. A well-calibrated model gives PIT values spread evenly between 0 and 1. If the predictions are too narrow, values pile up at the ends. If they are too wide, values thin out at the ends. The dashed line marks a perfectly even spread, and the band shows how far a calibrated model would wander from it by chance.

html`<div style="display:flex;flex-wrap:wrap;gap:12px">${OUT.map(v => {
  const d = ppc.pit[v], k = d.counts.length, e = d.n / k, sd = Math.sqrt(e * (1 - 1 / k));
  const bars = d.counts.map((c, i) => ({x1: i / k, x2: (i + 1) / k, c}));
  return Plot.plot({
    width: panelW, height: 170, marginLeft: 44, title: `${d.label}: PIT`,
    x: {domain: [0, 1], label: null, ticks: 5},
    y: {label: "↑ seasons", grid: true, domain: [0, Math.max(e + 3 * sd, ...d.counts) * 1.05]},
    marks: [
      Plot.rectY([{x1: 0, x2: 1, y1: e - 2 * sd, y2: e + 2 * sd}], {x1: "x1", x2: "x2", y1: "y1", y2: "y2", fill: REP, fillOpacity: 0.12}),
      Plot.rectY(bars, {x1: "x1", x2: "x2", y: "c", fill: "#9b9a94", inset: 0.5}),
      Plot.ruleY([e], {stroke: INK, strokeDasharray: "4,3"}),
      Plot.ruleY([0])
    ]
  });
})}</div>`

All four histograms are flat through the middle and thin at the ends. The predictions are a little too wide, which is why the 83% intervals hold 89% to 94% of seasons rather than 83%. The model is slightly cautious. It is not overconfident anywhere.

The third check matters most for this project. SAR and PAR rest on the team factor, so the model must reproduce how scoring changes with team strength. It must also not leave patterns across leagues or stages of a career. Each panel compares observed non-penalty goals per 90 (dark dots) with the model’s 83% predictive interval (blue bars).

{
  const panels = [["team_strength", "By team strength (season points per game vs league)"], ["league", "By league"], ["season_index", "By season observed"]];
  return html`<div style="display:flex;flex-wrap:wrap;gap:12px">${panels.map(([key, title]) => {
    const g = ppc.groups[key];
    return Plot.plot({
      width: key === "team_strength" ? Math.min(width, 640) : panelW, height: 230, marginLeft: 44, marginBottom: width < 640 ? 64 : 40, title,
      x: {domain: g.map(r => r.group), label: null, tickRotate: width < 640 && key !== "season_index" ? -30 : 0},
      y: {label: "↑ non-penalty goals per 90", grid: true, zero: false},
      marks: [
        Plot.ruleX(g, {x: "group", y1: "lo83", y2: "hi83", stroke: REP, strokeWidth: 10, strokeOpacity: 0.35}),
        Plot.dot(g, {x: "group", y: "observed", r: 4.5, fill: INK}),
        Plot.tip(g, Plot.pointerX({x: "group", y: "observed", title: r => `${r.group}\nobserved ${r.observed.toFixed(3)}\npredicted ${r.mean.toFixed(3)} (83%: ${r.lo83.toFixed(3)}–${r.hi83.toFixed(3)})\n${r.n} player-seasons`}))
      ]
    });
  })}</div>`;
}

Scoring doubles from the weakest fifth of teams (0.20 goals per 90) to the strongest (0.43), and every observed value falls inside its predictive interval. A single linear team factor on the log scale captures the whole gradient. The league effects and career curves leave nothing behind either: the observed rate sits inside the interval for every league and every season band.

The last check looks at players one by one. For each forward we add up his replicated goals across all his seasons and compare the result with his actual career total. Points on the diagonal match exactly. The grey bars are 83% intervals, and orange marks a player whose total falls outside his.

md`Of the ${ppc.careers.length} forwards, ${Math.round(ppc.career_coverage83 * 100)}% have career totals inside their 83% interval. The model shrinks each player's skill towards the average, so it should miss in both directions. It does. Harry Kane scored more than his interval allows: 208 goals against a prediction of 164 to 205. The model also has no career curves for individual players, so it cannot follow one forward's rise and fall. It reproduces the scoring of the typical forward better than the scoring of any single one.`

Taken together, the checks say the model describes these data well. Its predictive intervals run a little wide. It reproduces the distribution of every outcome, the effect of team strength and the differences between leagues. Within single careers it is coarser, because one skill score per player cannot track a decade of change.

Findings

Finishing hardly varies

The spread of each skill across forwards, as the posterior mean of \(\sigma_k\) on the log scale:

Shooter Fox Sniper Playmaker
0.29 0.29 0.05 0.32

Once you know how many shots a forward takes and where he takes them from, his finishing adds little. A forward one standard deviation above average at finishing scores about 5% more than his xG. A forward one standard deviation above average at shooting takes about 33% more shots. Analysts have long suspected that finishing above xG is mostly luck. Our model agrees.

The explorer therefore gives a forward three Sniper stars unless his 83% interval excludes zero. Haaland scored 103 non-penalty goals from 94.9 xG. The model still rates his finishing slightly below average, with an interval of −0.11 to −0.01 on the log scale. His strong teams account for the surplus. And forwards who get chances as good as his tend to convert them a little below their xG, as the next section shows.

Skills that go together

Posterior correlations between player skills, with 83% intervals:

Fox Sniper Playmaker
Shooter +0.21 [+0.15, +0.27] −0.19 [−0.39, +0.01] −0.17 [−0.23, −0.10]
Fox −0.66 [−0.82, −0.48] −0.61 [−0.65, −0.56]
Sniper +0.66 [+0.48, +0.83]

Creators take worse shots. The correlation between Fox and Playmaker, −0.61, is the clearest pattern in the data. A penalty-box striker lives on tap-ins and sets up little. A forward who drops deep to create shoots from further out.

Forwards with very good chances convert slightly below their xG. Perhaps that is a real pattern of skill. Perhaps Understat’s xG slightly overrates very good chances. A season-level model cannot tell which.

The correlation between Sniper and Playmaker deserves the least trust. Finishing varies so little that the model has little to go on, and this correlation is its worst-estimated quantity. Long-range specialists such as Payet and Suso, who both create and finish above their xG, probably drive it.

The team factor

Take a team one point per game above its league average, roughly a title contender. Its forwards gain on every skill:

Skill Effect (\(\beta_k\), 83% interval) Multiplier
Shooter 0.150 [0.135, 0.166] ×1.16 shots
Fox 0.241 [0.224, 0.257] ×1.27 xG per shot
Sniper 0.095 [0.074, 0.116] ×1.10 goals per xG
Playmaker 0.173 [0.154, 0.192] ×1.19 key passes

A forward on such a team scores about 1.6 times as many non-penalty goals as he would on an average team. Most of the gain comes from the quality of his chances. Their number rises less. The paper set out to remove exactly this confounding, and it is large.

The most skilled forwards

The top of the SAR ranking, in non-penalty goals per 90 above the replacement pool on a level field:

SAR 83% interval SAR − PAR
Darwin Núñez 0.29 0.18 – 0.41 −0.17
Cristiano Ronaldo 0.26 0.20 – 0.31 −0.23
Erling Haaland 0.24 0.17 – 0.31 −0.21
Robert Lewandowski 0.23 0.18 – 0.28 −0.28
Kylian Mbappé 0.22 0.17 – 0.29 −0.25
Pierre-Emerick Aubameyang 0.22 0.17 – 0.28 −0.07
Charlie Austin 0.22 0.14 – 0.32 +0.11
Lionel Messi 0.22 0.17 – 0.27 −0.26

From second to tenth the intervals overlap almost completely. Read the table as a group of about ten elite forwards. The order within it is mostly noise. Núñez comes first on fewer than 4,000 minutes, and his interval is the widest in the table. The paper made the same point about Haaland: a short career earns a wide interval.

Like the paper, we find that the best forwards gained most from their teams. Lewandowski, Messi, Mbappé, Suárez and Ronaldo gained 0.23 to 0.28 goals per 90 from theirs. At the other end, Charlie Austin, Teemu Pukki, Simy and Aleksandar Mitrović scored fewer goals than their skill implies, because they played for weak teams.

Messi ranks lower here than in the paper, for three reasons. We leave out penalties. His creating, the best among the top ten at 1.9 times a typical forward’s, adds nothing to his goals. And our window includes his two seasons at Paris Saint-Germain, at 34 and 35. The model has no individual career curves to set those seasons apart.

Careers

The average multiplier for each skill, by season observed. Season 1 is a player’s first season in the 2014/15–2023/24 window.

Season 1 Season 3 Season 5 Season 10
Shooter 1.04 1.02 1.00 0.94
Fox 0.94 0.98 1.02 1.07
Sniper 1.03 1.03 0.99 0.93
Playmaker 1.00 1.00 1.00 1.02

As forwards age, they shoot less often and from better places. Their scoring peaks around the third season observed, about 3% above average, and ends about 7% below it. The paper found a steeper hump.

The curve has a flaw. Seasons are counted from the start of our window, so Messi’s first season is 2014/15, his eleventh as a professional. The curves show how forwards changed over one decade. They are not age curves.

What remains

Understat’s per-match lineups would let us fit the paper’s model as written: goals per match, an ordered logistic likelihood, the home and pre-match points factors, and form within the season. We already hold the match ids we would need.

Age in place of seasons observed would fix the career curves. Curves for individual players would stop a veteran’s decline years from dragging down his estimated skill. That was the paper’s main reason for fitting them. We have not yet repeated the paper’s cross-validation against simpler models. And the Fox and Sniper skills rest on Understat’s xG model, so they inherit its errors, possibly including the negative correlation between them.
